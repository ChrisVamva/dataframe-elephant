## Per-idea notes

### C01 — 8.0 (pursue first)
- Strongest question in the set: it tests whether two rights actually compose in a repair shop, with a clear comparison (independent vs. authorized shops on diagnosis, time, cost, completion).
- Weak point is data. Repair records for connected appliances are thin, and the Repair Directive only had to be transposed by 31 July 2026, so most of the evidence will be interviews and shop-level records, not national datasets. I'd scope it as a qualitative-plus-small-sample study and say so up front.
- The brainstorm scores evidence 3/3; I'd give it 2/3 for that reason.

### C05 — 7.5
- Best novelty in the set and the most tractable: it is mostly desk research (national transposition texts, status tests, court decisions), so it doesn't depend on outcome data that doesn't exist yet.
- The tension is real: both directives hinge on who counts as a worker, and the Platform Work Directive's presumption of employment is rebuttable. The interesting output is a map of where national status tests diverge.
- Sharpen the concept into a single question (e.g. "in which member states does a rebuttable platform-employment presumption also unlock pay-disclosure rights?") to lift the specificity.

### C04 — 7.0
- Good question and a clear affected population, but the evidence trail has a timing problem: platform-work rules don't have to be transposed until 2 December 2026, so "documented appeal outcomes under the new rights" mostly won't exist.
- A workable fix is to use proxy evidence from existing routes (GDPR automated-decision cases, such as the Dutch platform-driver litigation) and treat the directives as the forward benchmark.
- C04 and C05 share a legal backbone; consider running them as one project with two work packages.

### C07 — 6.5
- Tractable legal crosswalk with clean boundaries (design and published plans, not outbreak outcomes).
- Risks: it is a well-covered area for legal scholars, so the contribution has to be the duty-allocation crosswalk rather than commentary. The Pandemic Agreement's operability also depends on its pathogen access and benefit-sharing annex, so check where that stands before committing; the anchor may have moved since the brainstorm.

### C02 — 5.5
- The anchor leans on Regulation 2024/1781, but product-specific ecodesign requirements under it mostly haven't started to apply, and the existing durability and repair rules sit in older product-specific measures. That makes "do durability rules lead to repair rather than replacement" premature as stated.
- It also overlaps C01's data needs without C01's sharper question. I'd either fold it into C01 as a secondary analysis or defer it until product-specific rules are in force.

### C10 — 5.0
- The brainstorm self-scores this 3/3 on evidence; I disagree. It fails for the same reason its own drop log gives for C12: local project records are inconsistent, and permit and inventory data are patchy.
- Conceptual gap: FERC Order 1920 governs regional high-voltage transmission planning, which rarely shares street excavation with lead service lines. The overlap that would matter for construction coordination is distribution-grid work, which the anchor doesn't cover.
- Anchor risk: check the current status of the EPA lead rule before relying on the 8 October 2024 finalization and ten-year replacement framing.
- Most creative pairing in the set, but the weakest causal link. Rework around distribution-level utility work, or drop.
