## 2. Universal Problems — 18 Cross-Domain Observations (No Internal Projection)

Per Protocol §1 (Universal Problems, 10–20 items). Each stated as if this repository never existed.

1. **Liability gap for autonomous decisions:** When an autonomous system causes harm, causation is distributed across designers, operators, and the system itself; no standard "decision audit trail" exists to support liability attribution.
2. **Actuarial impossibility for novel autonomy:** Insurance models depend on historical loss data; novel autonomous systems have no actuarial baseline, making premium-setting impossible and coverage gaps inevitable.
3. **Insurance market failure for systemic risk:** Autonomous systems may create correlated failures (e.g., fleet-wide software bugs); traditional insurance cannot price systemic risk without reinsurance markets.
4. **Transboundary water data incomparability:** Upstream and downstream jurisdictions monitor water quantity and quality with incompatible methods; no shared data contract exists for treaty enforcement.
5. **Hydrological treaty obsolescence:** Climate change alters hydrology faster than treaties update; water-allocation agreements based on historical flows become unenforceable within decades.
6. **Upstream/downstream enforcement asymmetry:** Downstream users bear costs of upstream decisions but have no enforcement mechanism; no portable dispute-resolution framework exists.
7. **Orbital slot allocation without sustainability:** ITU first-come-first-served orbital slot allocation has no environmental-impact requirement; debris-generation potential is not priced into access.
8. **Debris removal without property rights:** Active debris removal requires approaching another state's object; no legal framework permits intervention without consent, even for derelict objects.
9. **Post-mission disposal non-compliance:** Voluntary post-mission disposal guidelines have <50% compliance; no penalty mechanism exists for non-compliant operators.
10. **Credential jurisdiction-lock:** Professional and educational credentials are recognized within jurisdictions but not portable across borders; no universal mutual-recognition framework exists.
11. **Informal learning invisibility:** Experiential and informal learning (workplace training, MOOCs, self-study) has no credentialing pathway; skills are not captured in formal systems.
12. **Skills-ontology fragmentation:** Different industries and jurisdictions use incompatible skills taxonomies; a "data scientist" in one context may not match another; no interlingua exists.
13. **Traceability requirement divergence:** US FSMA Rule 204, EU traceability rules, and private standards (GS1, SQF) have different data requirements; multi-jurisdictional operators maintain parallel systems.
14. **Food fraud resilience gaps:** Economically motivated adulteration (EMA) exploits supply-chain opacity; point-solutions (testing at one node) are evaded by fraudsters who shift to unmonitored nodes.
15. **Allergen cross-contamination opacity:** Multi-product facilities produce both allergen-containing and allergen-free products; cleaning validation is inconsistent, and cross-contamination events are under-reported.
16. **Proctoring privacy-invasiveness:** Remote proctoring systems require continuous video, audio, and screen capture; privacy and accessibility concerns make them adversarial for students with disabilities.
17. **Credential verification fraud:** Paper and PDF credentials are easily forged; no interoperable, privacy-preserving verification standard exists for educational and professional credentials.
18. **Abyssal baseline data absence:** Deep-sea environmental baselines (biodiversity, sediment composition, ecosystem function) don't exist for most abyssal regions; impact assessment cannot distinguish mining effects from natural variation.

### Subject A — A Decision Audit Trail Standard for Autonomous Systems Liability

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Autonomous systems / liability law / insurance / product safety.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. EU Product Liability Directive revision (2024/2025) extends strict liability to AI-enabled products but leaves causation attribution undefined.
  2. US state-level autonomous vehicle legislation (California, Arizona, Michigan) requires event data recorders but no standardized format.
  3. IMO MASS Code negotiations for maritime autonomous shipping require "remote operational centers" but no decision-audit standard.
  4. Aviation black-box standards (flight data recorders, cockpit voice recorders) provide a structural template for decision capture.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. No standard **decision audit trail** captures the inputs, model version, confidence, override events, and operator interventions for autonomous systems across domains.
  2. Liability attribution requires a causal chain, but autonomous decision-making is distributed; no framework maps "who decided what when" for legal proceedings.
  3. Insurance markets cannot price risk without actuarial data; no standard data format exists for collecting loss events from autonomous systems.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Borrowed from aviation black-box practice and financial transaction logging. Define a **Decision Audit Trail (DAT)**: timestamped record of sensor inputs, model version, decision output, confidence score, operator override (if any), and post-hoc explanation. Standardize the format (schema), retention period, and access protocol.
- **Key Innovations (universal, not "new for us"):**
  - A **domain-agnostic DAT schema** applicable to autonomous vehicles, drones, maritime vessels, industrial robots, and medical devices.
  - A **causal-chain reconstruction protocol** that maps DAT records to liability-allocation rules (designer vs. operator vs. system).
  - An **actuarial data collection framework** so insurers can aggregate loss events across operators and build risk models.
- **External Validation:** Aviation black-box practice validates the "black box" concept; financial transaction logging validates the schema approach; EU Product Liability Directive validates the policy demand.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** Any autonomous system with legal liability (vehicles, drones, robots, medical devices) can implement the DAT.
- **Reproducible methodology:** Schema + causal-chain protocol + actuarial framework are public and implementable without proprietary tooling.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** Regulators get a standard for compliance; courts get a causal-chain tool; insurers get actuarial data; operators get a liability-defense tool.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Scope is bounded to decision capture, not liability-allocation rules (which are jurisdiction-specific).
- Requires operator cooperation; the schema is voluntary unless mandated by regulators.

### Subject B — An Interoperable Data Contract for Transboundary Water Monitoring

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Transboundary water governance / environmental monitoring / international law.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. UN Watercourses Convention (1997) requires "regular exchange of data and information" but no technical standard.
  2. OECD Principles on Water Governance (2016) recommend " timely, accurate, and comparable" data but no format.
  3. EU Water Framework Directive mandates monitoring but uses EU-specific indicators not transferable to other basins.
  4. WMO hydrological data standards (WMO No. 1235) provide a technical template but limited adoption outside meteorological services.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. Upstream and downstream jurisdictions monitor water quantity and quality with incompatible methods; no **interoperable data contract** exists for treaty enforcement.
  2. Hydrological treaties are static; climate change alters flows faster than treaties update; no mechanism exists for **dynamic treaty adjustment**.
  3. Downstream users bear costs of upstream decisions but have no enforcement mechanism; no portable **dispute-resolution framework** exists.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Adapt W3C data-interoperability practice and sensor-network standardization (SensorML, OGC standards) to a **Transboundary Water Data Contract**: standardized parameters (flow, quality, temperature, sediment), sampling protocols, metadata requirements, and a shared data-exchange format (JSON-LD with W3C PROV provenance).
- **Key Innovations:**
  - A **basin-agnostic data contract** applicable to any transboundary river, lake, or aquifer, with optional extensions for basin-specific parameters.
  - A **dynamic-treaty adjustment protocol** that triggers treaty review when hydrological indicators exceed thresholds (e.g., flow drops below X% of historical mean for Y years).
  - A **dispute-resolution trigger** based on data-contract violations (e.g., missing data, inconsistent sampling) rather than substantive disagreement.
- **External Validation:** W3C data standards validate the interoperability approach; OGC sensor standards validate the technical template; UN Watercourses Convention validates the policy frame.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** Any transboundary water system (rivers, lakes, aquifers) can adopt the data contract.
- **Reproducible methodology:** Data contract + dynamic-treaty protocol + dispute-resolution trigger are public and implementable by any basin organization.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** Basin organizations get a data standard; treaty negotiators get a dynamic-adjustment tool; dispute mediators get an objective trigger.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Requires political agreement to adopt the data contract; the technical standard is voluntary unless mandated by treaty.
- Scope excludes water-allocation policy (how much water each country gets), which is a political decision.

### Subject C — A Property-Rights Framework for Active Debris Removal

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Space governance / orbital sustainability / international law.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. ITU Radio Regulations allocate orbital slots and spectrum by first-come-first-served; no environmental-impact requirement.
  2. IADC Space Debris Mitigation Guidelines (2007) are voluntary; post-mission disposal compliance is <50%.
  3. Outer Space Treaty (1967) prohibits national appropriation of outer space but is silent on debris ownership.
  4. ESA ClearSpace-1 mission (planned 2025) will demonstrate active debris removal but requires consent from the object's registering state.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. Active debris removal requires approaching another state's object; no legal framework permits intervention without consent, even for derelict objects posing collision risks.
  2. Orbital slots are allocated without debris-generation pricing; no economic mechanism internalizes the externality.
  3. Post-mission disposal is voluntary; no penalty mechanism exists for non-compliant operators.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Adapt maritime salvage law (International Convention on Salvage, 1989) and environmental-liability frameworks (polluter-pays principle) to a **Space Debris Property-Rights Framework**: define conditions under which a derelict object may be removed without consent (e.g., collision risk exceeds threshold, object is non-functional for >X years), establish a "salvor" right to compensation, and create a debris-removal fund financed by orbital-slot fees.
- **Key Innovations:**
  - A **derelict-object threshold** (non-functional for X years, collision risk > Y) that triggers "salvage" rights without consent.
  - A **debris-removal fund** financed by orbital-slot fees or launch taxes, compensating salvors and funding active removal missions.
  - A **post-mission disposal bond** required at launch, refundable upon successful disposal, creating an economic incentive for compliance.
- **External Validation:** Maritime salvage law validates the "salvor rights" concept; polluter-pays principle validates the economic mechanism; ITU orbital-slot allocation validates the financing source.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** The framework is specific to space but the economic mechanism (bonds, funds, salvage rights) transfers to other environmental commons (oceans, atmosphere).
- **Reproducible methodology:** Derelict-object threshold + debris-removal fund + disposal bond are implementable by international bodies (ITU, UNCOPUOS) without requiring treaty revision.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** Space agencies get a legal framework for debris removal; insurers get a risk-pricing tool; regulators get a compliance incentive.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Requires international agreement (UNCOPUOS, ITU); the framework is a proposal, not existing law.
- Scope excludes military space activities, which are governed by separate legal frameworks.

### Subject D — A Universal Skills Ontology for Credential Portability

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Labor markets / education / digital identity / workforce development.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. W3C Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Verifiable Credentials (VCs) provide a technical standard for portable, privacy-preserving credentials.
  2. UNESCO Global Convention on the Recognition of Qualifications (2019) provides a legal framework for mutual recognition but no technical standard.
  3. ESCO (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations) provides a skills taxonomy for the EU labor market but is not transferable outside Europe.
  4. O*NET (US) and ISCO (international) provide occupational classifications but not skills ontologies.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. Skills ontologies are jurisdiction-locked; a "data scientist" in one context may not match another; no **universal skills interlingua** exists.
  2. Informal and experiential learning (workplace training, MOOCs, self-study) has no credentialing pathway; skills are invisible to formal systems.
  3. Credentials are not portable across borders; verification requires manual processes; no automated, privacy-preserving verification standard exists.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Adapt W3C DID/VC standards and ontology-engineering practice (OWL, SKOS) to a **Universal Skills Ontology (USO)**: a machine-readable, multilingual skills taxonomy with defined skill levels, prerequisites, and evidence types. Pair the ontology with a **Verifiable Credentials for Skills (VCS)** standard so credentials are portable, privacy-preserving, and automatable.
- **Key Innovations:**
  - A **domain-agnostic skills ontology** applicable to any industry, with extensions for domain-specific skills (e.g., healthcare, construction).
  - A **credentialing pathway for informal learning** that accepts multiple evidence types (portfolio, assessment, peer review) and maps them to ontology-defined skills.
  - A **privacy-preserving verification protocol** based on ZK-proofs so credential holders can prove they possess a skill without revealing the credential itself.
- **External Validation:** W3C DID/VC standards validate the technical approach; UNESCO convention validates the policy frame; ESCO validates the ontology structure.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** Any labor market, education system, or credentialing body can adopt the USO and VCS.
- **Reproducible methodology:** Ontology + credentialing pathway + verification protocol are public and implementable without proprietary tooling.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** Workers get portable credentials; employers get verifiable skills data; education providers get a standard for credentialing informal learning.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Requires adoption by credentialing bodies and employers; the standard is voluntary unless mandated by regulators.
- Scope excludes labor-market policy (wages, working conditions), which is a political decision.

### Subject E — A Network-Resilience Metric for Food-Fraud Prevention

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Food safety / supply-chain security / public health.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. FSMA Rule 204 (US) requires traceability for high-risk foods but uses a "one-up, one-down" model that does not capture network effects.
  2. EU traceability regulations (General Food Law) require "one-step forward, one-step back" but no network-wide visibility.
  3. GS1 traceability standards (EPCIS) provide a technical format for event data but no resilience metric.
  4. Food-fraud vulnerability assessment tools (VACCP, TACCP) identify high-risk nodes but do not model network resilience.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. Food-fraud prevention focuses on individual nodes (testing, auditing) but does not model **network resilience** — how fraud propagates through the supply chain.
  2. Traceability requirements are jurisdiction-specific; multi-jurisdictional operators maintain parallel systems; no **interoperable traceability contract** exists.
  3. Allergen cross-contamination is under-monitored; no standard exists for **cross-contamination risk scoring** in multi-product facilities.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Adapt network-science resilience metrics (robustness, redundancy, modularity) and epidemiological propagation models (R0, attack rate) to a **Food-Fraud Network Resilience Metric (FFNRM)**: quantify how fraud or contamination propagates through a supply-chain network, identify critical nodes, and measure the network's ability to absorb shocks.
- **Key Innovations:**
  - A **network-resilience metric** computable from traceability data (who supplies whom, volumes, frequencies) that quantifies fraud/contamination propagation risk.
  - A **critical-node identification protocol** so operators can prioritize monitoring and testing at high-risk nodes.
  - A **cross-contamination risk scoring** method for multi-product facilities based on product adjacency, cleaning frequency, and allergen potency.
- **External Validation:** Network-science resilience metrics validate the technical approach; epidemiological propagation models validate the fraud-propagation analogy; GS1 EPCIS validates the data format.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** Any supply chain with fraud or contamination risk (food, pharmaceuticals, electronics) can apply the FFNRM.
- **Reproducible methodology:** Network-resilience metric + critical-node protocol + cross-contamination scoring are public and implementable from traceability data.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** Food operators get a risk-prioritization tool; regulators get a network-wide visibility metric; insurers get a risk-pricing input.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Requires traceability data; the metric is only as good as the data quality.
- Scope excludes food-safety microbiology (pathogen growth, shelf-life), which is a separate scientific domain.

### Subject F — A Privacy-Preserving Assessment Integrity Framework for Education

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Educational assessment / privacy / accessibility / credentialing.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. Remote proctoring systems (Proctorio, ProctorU) use video, audio, and screen capture but raise privacy and accessibility concerns.
  2. Oral examinations are used in professional certification (medical boards, PhD defenses) but are resource-intensive and not scalable.
  3. IMS Global Caliper Analytics provides a standard for learning-data interchange but no assessment-integrity framework.
  4. EU AI Act education provisions restrict automated decision-making in education but do not define acceptable assessment methods.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. Remote proctoring is adversarial (privacy-invasive, accessibility barriers) and ineffective (AI-assisted cheating evolves faster than detection).
  2. Credential verification has no interoperable standard; employers cannot verify credentials without manual processes.
  3. Learning outcomes are not comparable across modalities (in-person, online, hybrid); no **modality-agnostic assessment framework** exists.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Adapt cryptographic zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) practice and oral-examination design to a **Privacy-Preserving Assessment Integrity Framework (PPAIF)**: define assessment methods that verify competence without invasive monitoring, use ZKPs to prove credential validity without revealing the credential, and establish modality-agnostic assessment standards.
- **Key Innovations:**
  - A **ZKP-based credential verification protocol** so credential holders can prove they possess a credential without revealing the credential itself (privacy-preserving).
  - An **oral-examination revival protocol** with structured rubrics, standardized prompts, and remote-delivery methods that scale beyond PhD defenses.
  - A **modality-agnostic assessment rubric** that defines competence in terms of observable behaviors, not delivery mode, so in-person and online assessments are comparable.
- **External Validation:** ZKP cryptography validates the privacy-preserving approach; oral-examination practice validates the scalability challenge; IMS Global standards validate the data-interchange layer.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** Any educational institution, credentialing body, or employer can adopt the PPAIF.
- **Reproducible methodology:** ZKP protocol + oral-examination rubric + modality-agnostic standards are public and implementable without proprietary tooling.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** Students get privacy-preserving assessments; employers get verifiable credentials; regulators get an accessibility-compliant framework.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Requires cryptographic infrastructure (ZKP libraries); adoption depends on technical capacity.
- Scope excludes pedagogical design (how to teach), which is a separate educational domain.

### Subject G — An Abyssal Baseline Data Standard for Deep-Sea Mining Impact Assessment

#### External Context
- **Domain:** Deep-sea mining / environmental governance / marine biology / international law.
- **Established Solutions:**
  1. International Seabed Authority (ISA) is negotiating exploitation regulations (2025–2026) but has not finalized environmental-impact-assessment (EIA) requirements.
  2. BBNJ Agreement (Biodiversity Beyond National Jurisdiction, 2023) requires environmental impact assessments for activities in areas beyond national jurisdiction but no technical standard.
  3. CBD (Convention on Biological Diversity) EIA principles provide a general framework but no deep-sea-specific baseline standard.
  4. Scientific deep-sea sampling programs (e.g., JPI Oceans BASE project) collect baseline data but use inconsistent methods.
- **Gaps Identified:**
  1. Environmental baselines (biodiversity, sediment composition, ecosystem function) don't exist for most abyssal regions; impact assessment cannot distinguish mining effects from natural variation.
  2. Sampling methods are inconsistent across scientific programs; no **standardized baseline data protocol** exists for deep-sea ecosystems.
  3. Deep-sea disturbance may be irreversible (centuries to millennia for recovery); no **irreversibility threshold** is defined for decision-making.

#### Proposed Approach
- **Methodology:** Adapt terrestrial EIA baseline standards (NEPA, EU EIA Directive) and marine-sampling protocols (UNESCO IOC oceanographic methods) to an **Abyssal Baseline Data Standard (ABDS)**: define required parameters (biodiversity, sediment chemistry, ecosystem function), sampling methods (grab samples, trawls, imaging), metadata requirements, and data-sharing protocols for deep-sea environmental baselines.
- **Key Innovations:**
  - A **standardized baseline data protocol** applicable to any abyssal region, with defined parameters, sampling methods, and metadata requirements.
  - An **irreversibility threshold framework** that defines when deep-sea disturbance is "effectively irreversible" (e.g., recovery time > X years) and triggers precautionary decision-making.
  - A **data-sharing mandate** so baseline data collected by mining contractors is publicly available for independent verification.
- **External Validation:** Terrestrial EIA standards validate the baseline-data approach; UNESCO oceanographic methods validate the sampling protocols; CBD principles validate the precautionary threshold.

#### Expected Impact
- **Cross-domain applicability:** Any deep-sea activity (mining, bioprospecting, cable laying) can apply the ABDS.
- **Reproducible methodology:** Baseline protocol + irreversibility threshold + data-sharing mandate are public and implementable by scientific programs and regulators.
- **Actionable for external stakeholders:** ISA gets a technical standard for EIA; scientists get a sampling protocol; NGOs get a verification tool.

#### Limitations (External Only)
- Requires scientific consensus on parameters and methods; the standard is a proposal, not adopted law.
- Scope excludes mining-technology regulation (how to mine), which is a separate engineering domain.
