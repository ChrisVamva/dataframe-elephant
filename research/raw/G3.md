**Nanopublication provenance formats** (likely what was meant by “nanobubble”; physical nanobubble literature is unrelated to the research-to-data provenance discussion).

Nanopublications are a Linked Data / RDF format for publishing atomic scientific claims together with their provenance and metadata as self-contained, citable, machine-interpretable units. They address the need to attach provenance at the level of individual assertions rather than whole documents or datasets.

### Core structure (standard model)

A nanopublication consists of four named RDF graphs (using TriG or equivalent serialization):

1. **Head graph** – Identifies the nanopublication and links the other graphs.
2. **Assertion graph** – The atomic claim (one or more RDF triples). Example: a gene–disease association or any formal statement.
3. **Provenance graph** – How the assertion was generated (“how this came to be”). Links back to the assertion graph identifier. Typical content uses the W3C PROV Ontology (PROV-O) plus related vocabularies (PAV, Dublin Core, etc.): derivation (`prov:wasDerivedFrom`), attribution (`prov:wasAttributedTo`), methods, timestamps, source documents/DOIs.
4. **Publication Info graph** – Metadata about the nanopublication itself (creator, creation time, license, version, topic).

Formal schema (namespace `http://www.nanopub.org/nschema#`):

- `np:Nanopublication`
- `np:hasAssertion`, `np:hasProvenance`, `np:hasPublicationInfo` (functional properties)
- Assertion / Provenance / PublicationInfo are subclasses of `rdfg:Graph`.

Trusty URIs (content-hash based) make nanopublications immutable and verifiable.

**Provenance requirements (from guidelines):**
- MUST contain a link from the provenance graph to the assertion graph identifier.
- SHOULD include generation method, agents (authors, institutions, funding), timestamps, and source links.
- Common predicates in real deployments: `prov:*` (near-universal), `pav:*`, `dc:*`, `rdfs:*`.

### Extended model for multi-source / aggregated assertions (PROV-K)

Standard nanopublications assume a single primary source. For claims produced by aggregation, information extraction, or truth-discovery systems (multiple supporting/conflicting pieces of evidence), an extension adds a fifth named graph:

- **Knowledge Provenance graph** – Tracks the ensemble of evidence, supporting vs. conflicting sources, truth values, reliability criteria, and trust relationships.

This is formalized by the **PROV-K ontology** (extension of PROV-O, published 2025, available on Zenodo and at https://prov-k.dei.unipd.it/ontology/). Key elements include:

- Propositions and their truth values (`true` / `false` / `unknown`) with timestamps and certainty degrees.
- Supporting and conflicting evidence links.
- Trust relationships (agent–agent or agent–proposition).
- Reliability / sufficiency / consistency criteria (domain-specific extensions possible).
- Digital signatures and information sources.

The extension is backward-compatible: existing nanopublications remain valid; the knowledge-provenance graph is optional. Real-world application: ~197k gene-expression–cancer associations from the CORE system were published as extended nanopublications.

### Serialization and tooling notes

- Preferred syntax: TriG (compact named-graph RDF).
- Queryable with SPARQL over quad stores.
- Ecosystem includes libraries, command-line tools, and large published collections (tens of millions of nanopublications, especially in life sciences).
- Related vocabularies frequently mixed in: PROV-O, PAV (Provenance, Authoring and Versioning), PRV, Dublin Core, SIO (for domain assertions).

### Position in the research-to-data workflow

| Stage                  | Role of nanopublication / PROV-K                          |
|------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------|
| Extraction             | Capture extracted claims as assertions                    |
| Structured representation | Atomic, typed claims with explicit schema               |
| Normalization / Database | Immutable, citable units with lineage                   |
| Provenance / Evidence  | Core strength – fine-grained source lineage & trust       |
| Interpretation / New research | Citable micro-publications that can be aggregated, queried, and reused |
| Agent iteration        | Suitable target format for extraction or research agents that must emit verifiable claims |

### Trade-offs and limitations

- **Strengths**: Atomicity, built-in provenance, citability, FAIR-oriented, machine-interpretable, immutable via trusty URIs, extensible for multi-source cases.
- **Limitations**: Overhead for very simple claims; requires RDF tooling; standard model is single-source-centric (hence PROV-K); adoption outside life sciences / Linked Data communities is still limited; truth-value and trust modeling in PROV-K is relatively new (2025) and domain extensions are needed for full reliability criteria.
- **Alternatives / related**: Plain PROV-O on larger graphs, nanopublication-like patterns in other knowledge-graph systems, or simpler JSON-LD provenance wrappers. PROV-K can also be used independently of the full nanopublication container.

### Evidence confidence

- **High** for the core four-graph structure, schema, and PROV-O usage (official guidelines, multiple independent analyses of large corpora).
- **High–Medium** for the PROV-K extension and multi-source knowledge provenance (primary 2025 paper + ontology release; real deployment of ~197k instances).
- Physical “nanobubble” literature (ISO fine-bubble terminology, water-treatment papers, etc.) has no relevance to provenance formats in this context.
