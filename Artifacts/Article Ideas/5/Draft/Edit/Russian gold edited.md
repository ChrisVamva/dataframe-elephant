# The $4.3 Billion Detour: Where Russian Gold Went the Year the West Banned It

In the first year after the West moved to ban Russian gold, one country bought 75.7 tonnes of it — about $4.3 billion worth. Not China, not Turkey: the United Arab Emirates. A Reuters analysis of roughly a thousand shipment records found that between February 24, 2022 and March 3, 2023, the UAE's Russian gold imports ran at nearly sixty times their 2021 level, when the total was just 1.3 tonnes for the whole year.

Every gram of that trade was legal. The G7 ban stops *G7 parties* from buying new Russian gold; it says nothing about Dubai, Istanbul, or Shanghai. What makes the number remarkable is what it measures: not smuggling, but distance. Almost the entire export flow of the world's most sanctioned commodity redirected, in twelve months, through three non-G7 jurisdictions — the UAE, China, and Turkey together accounted for 99.8% of Russian gold exports in the shipment data.

Here is the question that number cannot answer, and the one this article follows: what happens to all that gold next? Because a bar of gold carries no memory. Its "origin" is whatever the next certificate says it is — and the one industrial step every gold bar must pass through, refining, is also the one step that erases where it came from. The ban's first year showed, in customs data sharp enough to read, how a commodity can change nationality without ever changing its story.

---

## The number: a trade rerouted, not reduced

Start with what the shipment records actually show, because the picture is unusually clear. The Reuters analysis (May 2023) traced about 1,000 shipments from the day of the invasion to early March 2023. The UAE's intake ran from 1.3 tonnes in 2021 to 75.7 tonnes — $4.3 billion — in the year that followed, and it did not act alone: the UAE, China, and Turkey together absorbed 99.8% of Russian gold exports.

Two things stand out. First, the speed: the UAE's Russian gold trade grew nearly sixtyfold in roughly twelve months — the infrastructure of scale (traders, refiners, logistics) assembled almost overnight, with more than a hundred named non-sanctioned buyers in the records. Second, the concentration: 99.8% through three jurisdictions means the rerouting was not a diffuse leak but a small number of high-volume corridors.

None of this was hidden. These were legal purchases by legal buyers, recorded in customs data by both sides. The London market had locked Russian gold out; Dubai and Istanbul and Shanghai did not. On its own terms, that is just sanctions geometry — a ban covering some buyers moves trade to the others.

The interesting part is not where the gold went. It is what "went" means for a metal that intends to come back.

**The takeaway so far:** the first year did not reduce Russian gold exports; it relocated their last declared stop. The 75.7 tonnes is not an evasion number — it is a *transit* number, and transit is where origin paperwork gets rewritten.

## The vanishing: when the official flow hits zero

Now fast-forward one year. Investigations into the period after April 2023 report something odd: officially, Russian gold essentially stops being exported outside the EAEU — Russia's own customs union with Armenia, Belarus, Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. Exports to the rest of the world fall to effectively nothing.

And yet Armenia — a country of under three million people — starts importing gold at a magnitude that investigators note rivals its entire GDP. That gold has no plausible domestic use at that scale. The obvious reading, and the one the investigation draws, is that Russia's gold is entering the EAEU, being re-exported onward with new paperwork, and losing its nationality at the border.

This part of the story deserves a health warning, because it rests on different evidence than the first year's numbers. The opening figures are customs records — the strongest kind of trade data there is, reported by both sides of each transaction. The Armenia re-routing is an investigative finding from a single outlet, and no second independent dataset has corroborated it. It comes with a built-in falsifier: if Armenian customs data showed those imports matched by domestic consumption, the story would collapse. That audit hasn't been run publicly — but nobody should treat the corridor as proven either.

What can be said without over-reaching: after April 2023, the *declared* origin of Russian gold entering world markets can no longer be traced through Russian export statistics, and at least one credible investigation reports a re-labelling corridor through an EAEU member. The metal did not stop moving. Its paperwork did.

**The takeaway:** the ban's second year moved the problem from "where is Russian gold going" to "what is this gold, wherever it's from" — a question no import statistics answer, because statistics follow declarations and gold follows refineries.

## The refinery pool: where origin goes to die

Why is gold the easiest major commodity to re-paper? Because of how it is made sellable.

Raw gold — doré, dust, scrap — arrives at a refinery from dozens of mines, in inconsistent purity. The refinery melts it all together, assays the pool, and casts standardized bars. By the time a kilobar is stamped with a serial number, the metal in it is a mixture of every source that fed the melt that week. The bar carries a *refinery* identity, not a *mine* identity. "Refined and stamped" is a manufacturing claim; it was never an origin claim.

The industry knows this, and built its trust model on the refinery instead: LBMA good-delivery accreditation means the refinery follows the right process. The stress test came in 2023. An ABC Four Corners and Australian Financial Review investigation found the Perth Mint had sold "doped" gold — diluted with silver and copper — to the Shanghai Gold Exchange, potentially triggering a recall of some A$9 billion in bars. The LBMA opened an incident review. The Mint's own board review reportedly found about 1,500 US customer accounts with potential compliance issues. And by November 2023 the Mint had signed an enforceable undertaking with AUSTRAC, Australia's anti-money-laundering regulator. The accreditation system's first big public test was not about origin at all — it was about whether one of its flagship refiners could be trusted on process, and the answer was "only with outside pressure."

For this story, the Perth Mint matters for a structural reason, not a scandal reason: it is documented proof that once doré from many sources is blended in a refinery pool, mine-level provenance is destroyed *chemically*, not just procedurally. No audit, however thorough, can un-mix a melt. If some of that doré was Russian and some was not, the output is neither and both — and every downstream certificate that says otherwise is describing the paperwork, not the metal.

This is the mechanism the detour relies on. Russian gold legally refined in a third country is no longer distinguishable — physically — from gold refined anywhere else. What happens at the refinery gate does not need to be hidden; the pool does the work.

**The takeaway:** a refinery is the perfect laundering step not because anyone breaks rules there, but because the industrial purpose of the place is to erase input identity. The ban regulates declarations. The refinery regulates nothing, because there is nothing left to regulate.

## Gold is the template, not the exception

If you think this is a gold-market quirk, look at the same pattern across other regulated inputs — because every documented laundering case in this research reduces to the same move: a lawful intermediary step converts a physical-origin question into a paperwork transaction.

- **Coltan.** UN Group of Experts reporting documents at least 150 tonnes of coltan fraudulently exported from M23-held territory in the eastern DRC to Rwanda in 2024 — part of Rwandan mineral exports exceeding $1 billion a year that cannot be explained by the country's own mine output. The minerals crossed a border and acquired a new declaration; the audit system was fed, not bypassed.
- **Cobalt.** Roughly a fifth to a third of Congolese cobalt output comes from artisanal mines, and researchers find it is blended into industrial feedstock such that buyers' attempts to separate the two are, in their word, futile. Same pool, different metal.
- **Polysilicon.** The US Department of Labor states plainly that solar-grade polysilicon is mixed, melted and cast into ingots in ways that make tracing to Xinjiang "extremely challenging" — which is why America's forced-labour import ban had to be written as a presumption ("wholly or in part"), not a test.
- **Diamonds.** The G7's answer for diamonds was to bind stones to certificates through the Antwerp verification node — progress, and instructive: it covers only a size band of stones, and only where the chain of custody starts.

The general form, documented case by case: a transformation that is lawful in itself, an attestation that replaces physical evidence at the hand-off, and a jurisdiction outside the regime's reach. The metal does not need to be smuggled. It needs to be *processed* — somewhere the paperwork can legally restart.

**The takeaway:** Russian gold is the cleanest case study because customs data let us watch the detour happen in numbers. The same architecture underlies every regulated-input laundering story in the research, from coltan to polysilicon.

## The 99.8% box

**99.8% of Russian gold exports in the first year of the rerouting flowed to just three jurisdictions — the UAE, China, and Turkey.**

That number means the rerouting was corridor-shaped: a handful of hubs with refining capacity, legal access to Russian supply, and no sanctions exposure. It does *not* mean 99.8% of Russian gold was smuggled, laundered, or re-entered G7 markets — the shipment data shows only where gold was first sold after the invasion, not what became of it afterwards. The gap between those two questions is precisely the gap this article is about.

---

## What we don't know

- **"First year of the ban" is shorthand for a window that opens earlier.** The customs analysis runs from the February 24, 2022 invasion to March 3, 2023; the G7's commitment to ban new Russian gold was announced that June. Most of the 75.7 tonnes postdates the ban, but the window and the ban's first year are not identical periods — treat the numbers as *the first year of the rerouting*, which is what the data actually measures.
- **The Armenia corridor is a one-outlet finding.** The re-routing reports come from a single investigation; no independent dataset corroborates them, and the confidence rating is low. Its falsifier — Armenian customs data matching imports to domestic consumption — has not been publicly run.
- **Re-entry into Western supply chains is an inference, not a measurement.** Nothing in the research traces a specific bar of Russian origin through a third-country refinery into a G7 buyer. The mechanism is documented (refinery pools erase input identity); the specific flow is not. Treating "re-refined gold is back in London vaults" as established fact would overstate the evidence.
- **The Perth Mint case is about process, not origin.** The dilution and AML findings are documented; a related element circulating in 2023 coverage — gold reaching Iran-linked buyers through intermediaries — was not verifiable against a primary source and is deliberately left out.
- **The re-refining share is unmeasured.** No source this article draws on publishes what fraction of third-country refined gold originated in Russia after the ban. The honest statement is that the number is unmeasured, not that it is zero.
- **Records move.** The customs-analysis window closes in March 2023; enforcement, corridor shapes, and the G7 regime itself have kept evolving since. Re-verify against the controlling sources before relying on any figure here.

---

## The number to remember

**Two numbers, one metal.** 75.7 tonnes, $4.3 billion: the volume of Russian gold that legally rerouted through non-G7 hubs in the ban's first year — customs-record fact. 99.8%: the share that went through just three jurisdictions — the shape of the detour. And between the two sits the mechanism that makes the detour durable: a refinery pool that erases input identity as its ordinary industrial function.

The ban changed where gold was *declared*. Nothing in the regime — or in any current origin regime — changes what the metal itself can prove about where it was mined, because the metal cannot prove anything. That is not a loophole in the gold ban. It is the design gap every origin regime shares, and the gold market simply demonstrated it at $4.3 billion scale.

The next time a supply chain promises "sanctioned-origin-free," the follow-up question is the one the customs data forces: *free according to which document — and who melted the metal that document describes?*
